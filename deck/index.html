A participatory budgeting simulator that lets any citizen allocate a city's budget and see — transparently, with every factor shown — the projected impact on service outcomes, equity, and carbon emissions.

The Problem

City budgets get decided in rooms most people never see.

A mid-size U.S. city moves roughly $1.2B a year across a dozen departments. Residents get, at best, a bar chart in a PDF.

There's no way for a citizen — or a council member — to explore trade-offs before the vote:

  • What happens to equity if we cut the transit budget by 20%?
  • Does the road expansion actually raise emissions, or offset them?
  • Which single move buys the most service outcome per dollar?

The gap
Budgets are decided opaquely.
Consequences are lived publicly.

BudgetBallot closes that gap with a tool a resident can use in a browser.

The Differentiator

Transparent model, not generative model.

Most entries at this hackathon will be LLM wrappers with a chat box. That's fine — but for civic decisions, "the model said so" isn't a defensible answer.

BudgetBallot's math is documented, decomposed, and auditable. If a service outcome rises from 61 to 78, the UI shows you the four factors that produced it:

The Carbon Coupling

Budget vs. climate, in the same view.

Every service has a signed carbon elasticity. Some services reduce emissions when funded (transit, retrofits, parks). Some increase them (road expansion, larger vehicle fleets). Both are real. Both matter.

The trade-off is not an editorial claim — it's a Factor breakdown the user can click open. There is even a test whose sole job is to prove such trade-offs exist in the model (impact.test.ts).

Technical Architecture

Two engines, one model, one automated agreement.

The impact math lives in two places:

Client (TypeScript)
src/engine/impact.ts

Runs in the browser. Zero round-trip on slider changes. Instant.

Server (JavaScript)
server/engine.js

Canonical version. Powers POST /api/impact. Scriptable.

src/engine/parity.test.ts drives both implementations with the same inputs and fails loudly if they disagree. That's the safety net that makes it safe to iterate on the math.

The Narrator — Computed, Not Generated

A plain-English explanation that cannot hallucinate.

"You moved $67M from Road Expansion & Maintenance into Public Transit. Public Transit rises from 61 to 79 — the largest single gain in this scenario. Equity improves by 4.2 points, because the affected services carry heavy weight for underserved districts. Annual emissions fall by 12,540 tCO₂e, driven almost entirely by Public Transit."

Computed from the impact report, not generated by a language model.

  • No network. No API key. No LLM.
  • Works offline forever.
  • Same inputs → same words. Fully deterministic.
  • Cannot invent facts it doesn't have.

Sentence structure varies with which effect is decisive — a virtuous-case paragraph reads differently from a trade-off one. Tested in narrate.test.ts.

Scalability & Feasibility

Small footprint. Real path to production.

Today:

  • Static frontend + tiny Express API + JSON persistence.
  • No database, no queue, no cloud lock-in. Runs on any Node host.
  • Write-key auth + rate limiting on mutations. Reads intentionally public.

Roadmap:

  • Real municipal datasets + peer-reviewed emissions factors.
  • Per-district equity projections.
  • Federated write auth (OIDC / municipal SSO).
  • Multi-year, cumulative emissions view.
  • Publisher mode: shareable read-only permalinks with the narrated paragraph as social preview.
